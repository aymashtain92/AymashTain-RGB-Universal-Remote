Good instinct to question this — but "connect it to my PC" only fixes the lag if it changes the right part of the pipeline, and for this style of controller it might not.

Why it's actually late: MR Star (listed as "Miracles Star") is a generic Bluetooth-only remote app — it's a controller that connects to and manages devices purely over Bluetooth, reused across a ton of unrelated cheap LED products. On almost all controllers of this type, "music mode" isn't analyzed on your phone — the little inline controller box has its own onboard mic and does the beat detection locally. The app just flips music mode on and sets sensitivity/speed. So the lag comes from: sound traveling through air to that tiny mic, a crude onboard level/beat algorithm (no real FFT, more like an envelope follower), plus a smoothing transition so colors don't strobe harshly. None of that touches your phone's Bluetooth radio — which means just pairing the same "music mode" from a PC instead of a phone won't help, since the acoustic path is identical either way. 
mwm

Here's what would actually help, in order of effort:

1. Cheapest, no coding — feed it a clean signal instead of air
Swap the inline controller boxes for the "sound activated" variant with a 3.5mm line-in instead of (or alongside) a mic — same 4-pin connector into your existing strips, usually $5–10 each. Run a cable from your PC's headphone/line-out into it. This takes background noise and the acoustic path out of the equation entirely and lets the lights react to the direct signal instead of a delayed, muddied recording of it — that's specifically why these exist for DJ/PA use. Downside: you'd likely lose the app's color picker unless you find a hybrid mic+aux model, since most aux versions ditch Bluetooth.

2. More work, best result on your existing hardware — bypass "music mode" entirely
Keep your current controllers, but drive them yourself instead of trusting their onboard detection. Cheap BLE RGB controllers like this almost always use a short, simple write command — hobbyists reverse-engineer these constantly, and a typical example looks like a fixed byte sequence such as 56 RR GG BB 00 f0 aa for setting a color. To find yours: 
github

Install nRF Connect (Android/iOS), or enable Android's Bluetooth HCI snoop log, open MR Star, and watch the bytes it writes as you change colors.
Write a small script on your PC with bleak (cross-platform BLE) plus an audio loopback library (soundcard, or pyaudiowpatch on Windows) that reads the exact digital audio your PC is about to output, does its own lightweight beat detection, and writes color commands directly to the strip.
This is the only option that actually removes the delay rather than shrinking it — you're reacting to the same samples before they hit your speakers, not a mic's recording of them. Since you have 3 identical strips, you only reverse-engineer the protocol once and just open three BLE connections from the same script.

3. If you want to go further — WLED
Worth knowing since you have three strips to keep in sync: cheap ESP8266/ESP32 boards flashed with WLED (~$8–15 pre-made "analog RGB controller" boards, no soldering, same 4-pin connector) can replace these controllers. WLED's sound-reactive mode does real local FFT off a mic module with genuinely low, tunable latency, and its "Audio Sync" feature lets one board's mic broadcast analysis over WiFi so all three strips react in lockstep — solving both the lag and the multi-strip sync problem together. It also exposes a real API if you later want PC control.

Ah, that changes the read a bit — if it's already using the onboard mic (not the phone), the app's claim of low latency there is basically right in principle, since there's no Bluetooth round-trip or phone processing involved at all. So if it still feels late, the two most likely culprits are: the mic isn't getting a clean/loud enough signal where it currently sits (forcing the onboard detector to average/smooth more to filter noise), or the specific "dance pattern" you like uses a color fade/transition rather than a hard snap on the beat — which would look slightly behind no matter how fast the detection is, since that's baked into the effect itself.

Your plan (cluster all three near the PC/speakers) directly attacks the first cause, so it's worth doing. One quick check before you cut anything: if this is a simple analog RGB strip (just DC voltage per channel, no addressable pixels), the wire length barely matters. If it's actually an addressable strip that just gets marketed as "RGB" and the same cable also carries a data signal, keep any added length modest — a meter or two — or you risk flicker/glitching from signal degradation, not power issues.

1
Identify the two wires that matter
Open up the cable where it meets the control module. On these, it's almost always just red (+5V) and black (GND) doing anything — if you see green/white wires too, those are USB data lines these devices don't use, since all communication happens over Bluetooth, not USB. You can ignore or skip extending those.
2
Cut and splice with matching gauge
Cut the cable on the USB-plug side of the module (not the module side), and splice in extra wire of similar or slightly thicker gauge — red to red, black to black. Solder joints with heat-shrink over each one hold up much better than twist-and-tape if these are getting moved into a new spot, but quick-splice/crimp connectors work fine too if that's what you've got on hand.
3
Test one unit before touching the other two
Power up just the first extended module and confirm it boots, pairs, and reacts to sound normally before cutting into the second and third. If you've got red/black swapped, this catches it on one board instead of frying all three at once — cheap boards like this rarely have reverse-polarity protection.
4
Check your power source can handle three
If you're feeding all three off a single USB port or a cheap passive splitter, make sure it can supply enough current for three devices simultaneously. Separate ports, or a powered hub, is safer than daisy-chaining them off one weak 5V source.
5
Get the mic genuinely close to a speaker
Same room isn't enough — get each module's mic side as near as practical to an actual speaker or subwoofer driver. Bass transients are usually what these simple onboard detectors key off, so proximity to whichever speaker is putting out the low end tends to help the most.
6
Re-tune sensitivity after the move
A much louder, closer signal can oversaturate a sensitivity setting that was calibrated for a quieter, farther mic position — you may see flicker or over-triggering instead of lag. Back the sensitivity down in the app once everything's relocated and check it against the pattern you actually use.
